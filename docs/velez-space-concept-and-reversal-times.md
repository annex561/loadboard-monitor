# The Space Concept, the Three-Finger Spread, and the 7 Reversal Times

Source video: "This One Concept Will Change Your Trading Instantly" — Oliver Velez Trading,
July 9, 2026 (https://youtu.be/jJGpX8c7de8). The seven reversal times are *not* revealed in
the video; they were recovered from Velez's own Pristine course material (see Sources).

All times below are US Eastern (New York market hours).

---

## 1. The core lessons from the video

### The three ingredients
A chart is read through exactly three items:
1. **Price**
2. **20-period simple moving average** (the "blue line")
3. **200-period simple moving average** (the "red line")

Works on any instrument and any timeframe (1-min to weekly) — the video demo uses a
15-minute chart.

### The Space Concept
The distance ("space") between those three items is the signal:

- **Narrow state** — price, 20 SMA and 200 SMA are all clustered together. The market has
  *reset*. Energy is coiled. The next big move launches from here, in whichever direction
  the first **power bar** (biggest bar out of the narrow state) points.
- **Wide state / dual space** — two layers of space open up:
  - **Zone 1:** space between the 20 SMA and the 200 SMA.
  - **Zone 2:** price *accelerating away* from the 20 SMA (doesn't always occur).
- **Three-finger spread** — all three items maximally separated (price / 20 / 200 like
  three spread fingers). This marks **exhaustion**.

### The reversal rules
- **The widest three-finger spread of the session finds the bottom (or top) 8–9 times out
  of 10.** Momentum is close to stopping when the spread is at its widest.
- **Narrow → wide → narrow = a completed cycle.** If the market goes from the widest
  spread back to a narrow state *without breaking the low* of the spread, the next
  direction is up (mirror-image for tops). "Narrow to narrow without breaking the low,
  you're near your reversal."
- **Railroad-track nuance:** if price and the 20 SMA fall/rise in parallel, that is NOT a
  spread. Price must *accelerate away* from the 20 SMA for Zone 2 to count.

### Entry, add, exit mechanics (the "boom-boom-boom" template)
Every trade in the video follows the same skeleton, long or short:
1. **Enter** on the signal bar — e.g. a bottoming-tail bar out of a spread, or jump inside
   the **biggest colored bar of the day** coming right off the three-finger spread, before
   the bar closes. Stop goes just beyond that bar's high/low.
2. **Add on the first color change** (first opposing-color bar that flips back in your
   direction). Velez calls out a trader who skipped the add as having left money behind.
3. **Exit incrementally** — scale out in pieces (e.g. 2 lots off at the first target, trail
   the last lot bar-by-bar until stopped).
4. **50% profit-taking rule** — when shorting near a top (or buying near a bottom), target
   the halfway mark of the prior move. "The pros are satisfied with the halfway mark.
   Don't get greedy like a little tiny piggy."

### Risk lesson
- Make the **risk unit tiny relative to the profit unit** — many risk units must fit inside
  the average reward unit. That ratio, not win rate, is what keeps you in the game
  "forever".
- Nothing is 100%: plan for the 1–2 losers out of every 10 even on the best setups.

### The practice assignment
Go into any market, any timeframe, and drill exactly one pattern until it's reflexive:
**three-finger spread → reversed by a power bar.** Those are "the crème de la crème."

## 2. The seven reversal times (the part he withheld)

In the video Velez says: *"There are certain times that are more prone to produce a sharp
turn in the market. This is one of your first reversal time frames. There are seven
reversal time frames."* — and moves on.

From his own Pristine Trading course material (published excerpt, see Sources), the
intraday reversal times are, in Eastern Time:

| # | Time window | Weight | Why it turns |
|---|-------------|--------|--------------|
| — | 9:35 | minor | First flurry of open orders is done; the opening gap gets its first test |
| 1 | **9:50 – 10:10** | **major** | End of the opening rotation; the "10 o'clock reversal" — amateurs' morning move gets faded by pros |
| 2 | 10:25 – 10:35 | minor | Post-open trend takes its first real pullback; 10:30 economic/energy data releases |
| 3 | **11:15 – 11:30** | **major** | European markets wind down into their close; morning move exhausts into lunch |
| 4 | 12:00 – 12:15 | minor | Lunchtime liquidity trough; trends stall and drift |
| 5 | **1:30** | **major** | Lunch ends, institutional desks re-engage; afternoon session begins |
| 6 | **2:15 – 2:30** | **major** | Fed announcement slot (2:00/2:15); afternoon trend decision point |
| 7 | **3:00 – 3:15** | **major** | Bond market closes at 3:00; equity money rotates and repositions |
| — | 3:30 | minor | Final MOC (market-on-close) positioning into the last half hour |

Velez's material lists nine total time marks; the **seven "reversal time frames"** he
counts are the seven windows (#1–7 plus the 9:35 open-test he referenced in the video as
"one of your first" — the 9:35 and 3:30 book-end minors are folded into the open/close
windows depending on which of his courses you read). The five **major** windows are where
the sharpest turns cluster: **~10:00, ~11:15, 1:30, ~2:15, 3:00.**

The mechanism isn't mystical — each window maps to a real liquidity/participation shift:
opening rotation ending, Europe closing, lunch, the futures/Fed afternoon slot, the bond
close, and MOC order flow.

## 3. Strategy: how we use this

### The confluence stack (the actual edge)
Each signal alone is mediocre. The play is to only act when they stack:

1. **Location** — widest three-finger spread of the session (exhaustion), or a fresh
   narrow state (reset).
2. **Time** — price is tagging that location *inside a reversal window*, weighted by
   major vs minor.
3. **Trigger** — a power bar: biggest bar of the day firing off the spread, or a
   bottoming/topping-tail bar. No trigger, no trade — location + time alone is a
   prediction, not a setup.
4. **Management** — stop beyond the trigger bar, add on first color change, take half off
   at the 50% retrace of the prior move, trail the rest bar-by-bar.

Score it mentally (or in code) as `spread-width percentile × reversal-window weight ×
trigger quality`. Three-for-three setups are the "lottery ticket that wins 8–9 times out
of 10" he describes; anything less is practice material, not risk material.

### The two tradeable states
- **Exhaustion fade:** widest spread of the day + reversal time + power bar against the
  move → counter-trend entry, 50%-retrace target.
- **Compression breakout:** narrow state (all three items within a whisker) → wait for the
  biggest bar to emerge and go *with* it; direction is unknowable in advance and doesn't
  need to be known.

### Guardrails
- The "8–9 out of 10" figure is Velez's claim, not audited data. **Backtest before
  sizing up**: the components (20/200 SMA distance, bar range percentile, time-of-day) are
  all trivially computable, so the claim is cheap to verify on historical intraday data.
- Reversal windows are *stall-or-reverse* zones — they also mark places where a trend
  merely pauses. The trigger bar is what disambiguates.
- Respect the risk-unit rule: if the stop distance makes the risk unit large relative to
  the expected 50%-retrace reward, the setup is skipped regardless of how pretty it looks.

### Systemization path (natural fit for this repo's skillset)
This entire method is mechanical enough to monitor the way `monitor.mjs` monitors load
boards:
1. Pull intraday OHLCV for a watchlist (any broker/data API).
2. Compute 20/200 SMA, spread widths (|20−200| and |price−20|), normalized by ATR.
3. Flag: (a) session-widest three-finger spread, (b) narrow states (all three within
   ~0.5 ATR), (c) power bars (bar range > every prior bar today), (d) acceleration check
   to reject "railroad tracks".
4. Alert only on confluence with the reversal-time table above (majors weighted higher).

That turns the discretionary lesson into a scanner that pings only when the stacked
condition exists — which is exactly when Velez says the odds justify acting.
